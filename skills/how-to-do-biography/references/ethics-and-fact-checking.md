# Ethics and Fact-Checking

## Evidence Grades

Use these grades when planning, drafting, or reviewing claims.

- A: Multiple independent first-hand or contemporary sources support the claim. Use confident prose.
- B: One strong source, or several sources with shared origin. Attribute clearly and avoid overstatement.
- C: Retrospective memory, family lore, hostile testimony, anonymous report, or unsourced repetition. Usually exclude; if necessary, frame as disputed.
- D: No identifiable source. Do not use as fact.

When a claim matters to reputation, legality, medical history, sexuality, parentage, finances, abuse, crime, politics, or cause of death, require stronger evidence than ordinary narrative facts.

## Source Handling

- Treat the subject's own story as evidence of self-presentation as well as possible fact.
- Separate what happened, who says it happened, when they said it, and why they may have said it.
- Track whether evidence is contemporary or recollected later.
- If sources conflict, preserve the conflict when it matters. Do not smooth contradiction merely to improve pace.
- Use notes to expose uncertainty, variant accounts, source limitations, and method.
- Never cite a source you have not actually checked unless the prose explicitly says it is cited by another author.

## Privacy and Harm

Private material belongs in the work only when it helps explain the subject's actions, character, work, public role, or historical effect.

Before using sensitive private material, write a short internal rationale:
- What does this reveal that readers need to understand?
- Is there a less harmful way to make the same point?
- Is the evidence strong enough for the likely harm?
- Are living people affected?
- Would the passage still be necessary if it were less sensational?

Avoid:
- Voyeuristic detail.
- Single-source sexual, medical, or family accusations.
- Certainty about motives or psychology beyond the evidence.
- Punishing the subject for failing modern expectations without context.

## Defamation and Legal Risk

Flag claims involving crime, fraud, professional misconduct, abuse, addiction, sexual conduct, mental illness, parentage, betrayal, plagiarism, or cause of death.

For each flagged claim:
- Identify sources and evidence grade.
- Check whether the person or close associates are alive.
- Distinguish allegation, documented fact, inference, and opinion.
- Attribute contested claims in the sentence, not only in the notes.
- Consider legal review for publication-facing work.

## Writing Disputed Material

Use prose that matches the evidence:
- A-grade: "The records show..."
- B-grade: "According to..." or "The surviving evidence suggests..."
- C-grade: "A later account claimed..." followed by context or counterevidence.
- Unknown: "The evidence does not show..." or omit.

When two credible accounts conflict, name the conflict and explain why it matters. The goal is not a perfectly smooth story; the goal is a truthful account of a life as the evidence permits it.

## Fact-Check Table

For high-risk or central passages, create a table with:
- Claim.
- Source.
- Source type.
- Evidence grade.
- Corroboration.
- Counterevidence.
- Wording decision.
- Remaining risk.

Use this table before finalizing chapters, proposals, jacket copy, publicity text, or any passage likely to be challenged.
