# Biography Writing Workflow

## 1. Define the Project

Actions:
- Write a private motive note: why this subject, why now, and why this writer.
- Write a public agenda note: what question the finished biography answers for readers.
- Identify the intended audience: scholarly, general serious, fans, family, institutional, children/YA, or magazine readers.
- Name the book's implied promise: correction, rediscovery, moral inquiry, career portrait, intimate life, public life, or cultural history through one person.

Deliverables:
- Working thesis.
- Reader profile.
- Scope statement: years covered, domains included, domains excluded.
- Preliminary title or subtitle options.

Questions:
- What existing stories about this person need confirming, complicating, or overturning?
- What would make this biography necessary if another biography already exists?
- What evidence would force the agenda to change?

## 2. Map the Life

Actions:
- Create a timeline with dates, locations, relationships, works, crises, honors, failures, illnesses, migrations, and deaths.
- Mark "turning points" separately from "events." A turning point changes the subject's direction, self-concept, reputation, power, work, or relationships.
- Build a relationship map with at least two perspectives for important ties.
- Identify recurring pressures: ambition, class, money, belief, sexuality, illness, exile, rivalry, family duty, public role, or artistic work.

Deliverables:
- Timeline with evidence status.
- Relationship map.
- List of unresolved mysteries.
- List of likely chapter engines.

Questions:
- Which events reveal a pattern rather than merely fill a date?
- Which relationships changed the subject's choices?
- Where does the subject's own account conflict with other evidence?

## 3. Research the Subject

Actions:
- Build a source inventory: archives, letters, diaries, published work, interviews, photographs, legal records, financial records, institutional files, journalism, memoirs, oral histories, and prior biographies.
- Start writing while researching once the core timeline is stable; drafting reveals missing evidence.
- Track each claim in a notes system with source type, date, reliability, page or archive locator, and confidence.
- Separate contemporary evidence from retrospective memory.
- Look for counterevidence early. Do not let admiration or hostility set the conclusion.

Deliverables:
- Source log.
- Fact table for high-risk claims.
- Interview list and question bank.
- Missing-evidence list.

Questions:
- Is this fact supported by contemporary evidence, later memory, or repetition?
- What would an opponent of this interpretation cite?
- Which records are unavailable, destroyed, sealed, or biased?

## 4. Choose the Narrative Shape

Actions:
- Pick a structural principle: chronological with thematic chapters, frame narrative, crisis opening, career arc, relationship arc, investigation, or braided public/private life.
- Design the opening after enough of the draft exists to know the real center of gravity.
- Avoid beginning with birth unless birth itself is dramatic, contested, or thematically central.
- Use chapters as movements with a dramatic question, not as containers for years.
- Let each chapter end with pressure, consequence, or a new question.

Deliverables:
- Chapter architecture.
- Scene list.
- Opening options.
- Ending strategy.

Questions:
- What question pulls the reader through this chapter?
- Does this structure reveal causality, or only sequence?
- What can be omitted without weakening the life story?

## 5. Write Scenes and Analysis

Actions:
- Use scenes when evidence supports place, time, participants, action, and consequence.
- After private or vivid scenes, answer "so what?" Connect the detail to character, decision, work, reputation, or historical meaning.
- Attribute uncertainty in the prose instead of smoothing it away.
- Keep analysis close to evidence. Do not psychoanalyze beyond the record.
- Balance the subject's view with witnesses, documents, and historical context.

Deliverables:
- Draft chapters with source notes.
- Scene evidence notes.
- Running list of claims to verify.

Questions:
- Does this scene advance plot, reveal character, or deepen theme?
- Is the scene vivid because it is evidenced, or because the writer is embellishing?
- What is known, what is inferred, and what remains unknown?

## 6. Revise and Prepare for Publication

Actions:
- Run a structure pass: agenda, chapter order, pacing, repetitions, missing transitions, and ending.
- Run a fact pass: dates, names, quotations, locations, relationship claims, chronology, and source notes.
- Run an ethics pass for privacy, living people, accusations, medical details, sexuality, family trauma, and disputed motives.
- Run a prose pass to remove lazy adjectives, unsupported certainty, melodrama, and hindsight moralizing.
- Prepare an author's note when access, method, disputed evidence, or ethical choices need transparent framing.

Deliverables:
- Revised manuscript.
- Fact-check table.
- Risk register.
- Source note or author's note.

Questions:
- Is the conclusion the natural result of evidence, or the writer's prior agenda?
- Where does the prose imply more certainty than the sources allow?
- Which living people could be harmed, and is that harm justified by historical necessity?
