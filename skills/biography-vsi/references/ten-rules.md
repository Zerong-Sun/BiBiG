# Ten Rules for Biography

From Hermione Lee, *Biography: A Very Short Introduction* (2009). Each rule is a **possibility**, not an absolute — Lee shows how every rule is qualified in practice.

## 1. The story should be true

Biography concerns real people; the writer owes responsibility to truth. Yet dramatization, scene-setting, suspense, and experimental forms (Symons, Hamilton on Salinger, Ackroyd, Morris) blur fact and fiction. Even sober narratives face ambiguous or missing evidence. Untruths gain weight by repetition. Sometimes the honest form is **gaps and unanswerable questions**.

## 2. The story should cover the whole life

Readers expect cradle-to-grave coverage, but emphasis shifts (childhood, domestic life, posthumous reputation). Chronology is often broken: backward narratives, thematic structures, micro-period books (Hayter, Shapiro), dual mini-biographies (Wittgenstein's Poker). Total coverage is not invariable.

## 3. Nothing should be omitted or concealed

Modern Western readers expect openness, yet narrative requires selection. Victorian discretion on sex and "domestick privacies" has given way to openness — except when living relatives, libel risk, or recent death require care. Even "exhaustive" lives emerge from choices.

## 4. All sources used should be identified

Authentication through witnesses, letters, and citations has a long history; extensive footnoting is a 20th-century professional norm now under pressure from trade publishing. Unauthorized lives may rely on anonymous witnesses; some sources are legally or ethically sealed. Authentication is desirable, not always possible.

## 5. The biographer should know the subject

Johnson and Boswell valued intimate social knowledge. Personal connection is no longer required and can burden authorized projects. Distance can aid clarity.

## 6. The biographer should be objective

Partiality is historical; psychoanalytic and ideological biographies skew evidence. All biography bears the writer's position (history, nation, gender, class, belief). Shared profession or experience often links writer and subject. Loathing, revenge, and uncritical adulation both distort; **involvement plus detachment** is the aim.

## 7. Biography is a form of history

"No man is an island." The biographer must place the subject in an age without trivializing historical process or reinforcing hierarchy uncritically. Whose lives get told is itself political. Popular biography types reveal what a society values.

## 8. Biography is an investigation of identity

Represent the self through anecdote, scene, habit, decision, public performance — without forcing a single theory of human nature. Rival disciplines (psychoanalysis, fiction, philosophy) challenge biography's claims; borrow carefully.

## 9. Biography should be entertaining

Johnson insisted pleasure matters; Carlyle and others resisted "dull" lives. Entertainment must not erase complexity or truth. The contract with the reader includes pace, voice, and narrative energy.

## 10. Biography has a contract with the reader

Readers trust the writer to be honest about method, limits, speculation, and motive. Experimental and self-reflexive biographies make the contract explicit. Betraying reader trust damages the genre.

## Using These Rules

For a given project, ask per rule: **Does this rule apply? How is it qualified? What risk appears if we follow or break it?**
